It was my first [ICRA](https://2026.ieee-icra.org/) and it was an awesome experience. It's a 5-day conference: the first and last days are for workshops and the 3 days in between are for the main conference. It was a huge conference with 8,000 attendees, and it's expected to go over 10k next year. This year it was set up in [Wien](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wien), and I haven't seen a more beautiful city in my life. It has gorgeous, picturesque streets and vintage medieval buildings.

This time they introduced something called innovation workshops, where people from industry come to give talks on current industry adoption and what's state-of-the-art there. I attended my first workshop on the startup scene in Europe. [Robert](https://www.linkedin.com/in/robertneilmackenzie/) organized it pretty well; it was interactive, and he additionally brought startup founders to give practical case studies.

After lunch I went to the contact-rich manipulation workshop. It was unbelievable to see the classical community fight with and make serious jokes about the learning community. Currently, the consensus seemed to favour sampling-based methods like [MPPI](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICRA.2016.7487277) for control and planning.

The exhibition was the most interesting part of the conference, although it was mostly focused on actuators, hands, arms and humanoids. Industry is focused on getting actuators with better torque density and affordable robot arms. I saw a wide variety of dexterous hands, ranging from motor-driven to tendon-driven, costing up to 150k EUR. That's crazy for one hand (five fingers).

After talking to some of the booths, I found that patents and certification take a lot of time, and one quite difficult problem actuator-making companies face is winding copper at a smaller scale, which limits how torque-dense you can make smaller motors.

It was entertaining to see humanoid robots all around, but they lacked technical people at the booths, so I wasn't able to find out much about whether they were actually using end-to-end learning methods, imitation learning or pre-programmed moves. I guess that's usually the downside of these expos: you only find marketing people. The exception was the [Flexion](https://flexion.ai/) team, who had some failed demos. It was cool because companies usually don't have the courage to show when their policies fail. The founder told me it was probably because of the glass boundary and the many people around causing reflections that degraded the performance of their robot.

> In robotics, if they are showing you demos and nothing is failing anywhere, there's a very high chance it's a carefully designed demo. As of 2026, we're nowhere near solving real-world robotics deployment (safety and continual learning being a few of the primary reasons).

The main conference ran for 3 days and combined panel sessions, innovation talks and other sessions, making it practically impossible to attend even half of it. I spared some time to look at the posters. Around 2,000 papers were accepted, but personally I felt like a lot of submissions were okayish, highly niche in terms of application, and could have used stronger scientific review. It was a pleasure meeting [Matt Mason](https://mtmason.com/), [Aaron Johnson](https://www.cmu.edu/me/robomechanicslab/), [Preston Culbertson](https://pculbertson.github.io/) and [Jitendra Malik](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~malik/).

Alongside the conference, there are always private and official social events. I was glad to be invited to the [Lightwheel](https://lightwheel.ai/) party (Thanks to [Martin](https://www.linkedin.com/in/martin-elbs-12bab32/), where I met a lot of interesting people and learned to do a bit of socialising and introductions. The YRP social hour and the [RAS](https://www.ieee-ras.org/) members meeting were rather unexpected, but I liked them. At the RAS meeting they talked about the internals of how the conference was organized, the status of student communities, problems with paper reviews and all sorts of organizational things.

Just before the conference, I went to [Salzburg](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salzburg). The riverbank along the [Salzach](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salzach) has the best views, and if you go when it's rainy, everything turns lush green and feels almost exotic.
